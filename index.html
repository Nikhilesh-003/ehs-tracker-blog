Nothing expires unnoticed

A residential high-rise has to keep dozens of statutory tests, certificates and service contracts current. This tracker watches every one of them, escalates as deadlines approach and emails the right contractor each morning, using only the Microsoft 365 tools the team already had.

01 · Motivation

Why manual tracking was a risk

A building's environment, health and safety (EHS) obligations come with deadlines. Water tanks must be cleaned and tested, pools and cooling systems checked for Legionella, lift and facade-access contracts kept in force and fire systems covered by a valid Civil Defence maintenance agreement. Each item has its own test cycle, its own contractor and its own expiry date. If one lapses, the building is out of compliance.

Before the tracker, keeping on top of this was a weekly manual job:

Manual monitoringEvery system's sheet had to be opened and scanned for upcoming expiries. That was slow and easy to miss.
Reliance on memoryWhich contractor, which test, which deadline: the knowledge lived in people's heads.
Repetitive emailingReminders were written one by one for each system, taking hours every month.
No escalationA reminder went out once. If nobody replied, nothing followed it up.
No visibilityThere was no quick way to see what was overdue, due soon or waiting on a report.
Compliance riskOne missed date could mean a statutory breach, with operational and reputational cost.

The goal was simple to state: no certificate, test or contract should ever expire without the right person being told, repeatedly and with rising urgency, beforehand.

02 · Components

The stack

The tracker is built entirely from Microsoft 365 tools the organisation already licenses, so it needed no new software and no IT project.

LayerToolRole
Data & logicExcel OnlineHolds every test and contract date and works out expiry, days remaining and alert status, recalculated every day
StorageOneDriveKeeps the workbook in the cloud so the automation can reach it at any hour
AutomationPower AutomateOne scheduled cloud flow per building system, run every morning at 8:00 AM
DeliveryOutlookSends a colour-coded HTML email to the responsible contractor

The key design choice is where the intelligence lives. The alert status is decided in the workbook, in plain view of the team, not hidden inside the automation. Anyone opening the sheet sees exactly what the flow will act on, and the flows stay simple: read, check, send.

Every cycle is different. A water tank alone has three tests on three different clocks. Each test type also has its own reminder lead time. Contracts get a single renewal reminder 15 days out. Water tests use lead times matched to how long the laboratory needs to sample and report. Each system has its own table and its own flow, so a problem in one never stops the others.

The write-back closes the loop. Each row records when it was last emailed, so a flow that runs twice in a day, or a row that appears in two lists, never sends a duplicate.

05 · Inputs

What people enter, and what they don't

The tracker is only useful if keeping it up to date is effortless. The team fills in at most five fields per row. Everything else is calculated.

  1. Report or test dateThe date on the certificate or report from the contractor. This one date drives every calculation that follows.
  2. Status as on dateWhether the system was Healthy or Unhealthy at that test, kept for reporting.
  3. Scheduled dateWhen the contractor will visit to inspect or collect samples. Entering it pauses reminders while the visit and report are pending.
  4. On holdA Yes/No switch that pauses all emails for an item, for example while the contractor waits for parts.
  5. RemarksFree-text notes for people. The automation never reads them.

An automation is only as good as its data. Asking for one date instead of five calculated ones removes most of the room for error.

06 · Escalation

Six alert levels, rising urgency

As an item's expiry date gets closer, it moves through three stages. Each stage has a first notice and a daily follow-up in a gentler "kind reminder" tone, so there are six alert levels in all.

OKreminder window opens7 days leftexpiry day →
Requested

A polite first notice asking the contractor to schedule a visit.

Then Follow-up Requested daily until a visit is booked.

Urgent

A firmer notice when only a week remains.

Then Follow-up Urgent daily until it's confirmed or renewed.

Alert

A critical notice on or after the expiry date: the item is now out of compliance.

Then Follow-up Alert daily until a new valid date is entered.

Nobody has to decide when to escalate. The status moves up as the dates pass, and it drops back to OK as soon as a new report date is entered.

07 · Logic

The daily check

Reminders are only useful if they're relevant. A contractor who already has a visit booked shouldn't be chased, and a lab that has collected a sample shouldn't be asked to collect another. Every morning, each flow puts every row through the same checks, in order:

Is the item on hold?Skip
Is a contractor visit scheduled and still ahead?Scheduled
Has a sample been collected, and is the report still within its turnaround time?Awaiting report
Has a sample been collected but the turnaround time has passed?Report overdue email
What is the row's alert status? If OK, nothing to send.Pick template
Was this row already emailed today?Skip
Add the row to that contractor's email, send, and stamp today's date on the row.Send

The pause states appear in the workbook itself. Enter a contractor's visit date and the row turns Scheduled. Record that a sample was collected and it shows Awaiting report until the report comes in.

If the report doesn't arrive in time, the row moves to Report Overdue, which gets its own email because the request is different. The contractor has already been on site, and what's missing is paperwork. Sending them the usual "please arrange a visit" email would only cause confusion.

Knowing when not to send matters as much as sending. Each pause rule removes a type of email that would have taught contractors to ignore the tracker.

08 · Output

One clear email per system

Rather than one message per area, every affected area of a system is grouped into a single table, colour-coded by urgency, with a subject line that names the system and the action needed. These are real emails sent by the tracker, showing only the body:

The first notice asks the contractor to book a visit. Follow-ups open with "This is a kind reminder", so contractors can tell a first notice from a chase at a glance, and the row colour shows how urgent it is. Some systems add a column of their own: the HVAC email lists the system type, such as chilled water, next to each zone.

✓ · Summary

Key takeaways

Design

  • Keep the decision logic where people can see it.
  • Ask humans for as few inputs as possible, and calculate the rest.
  • One independent flow per system limits the damage of any single failure.

Notifications

  • Escalation should be automatic and tied to dates, not to someone remembering.
  • Pause rules matter as much as send rules.
  • One grouped email beats many small ones.

Operations

  • Different situations need different messages: a missing report isn't a missing visit.
  • Recording when each item was last notified prevents duplicates.
  • Colour coding makes the workbook a dashboard too.

Approach

  • Tools the organisation already owns are often enough.
  • Something the team can maintain in-house is more likely to survive.
  • Start with the risk you're removing, then measure the time saved.
? · Questions

Frequently asked questions

What is an EHS statutory compliance tracker?

It is a register of every inspection, test, certificate and service contract a building is legally required to keep current, such as water tank cleaning, Legionella and microbiological tests, lift and facade-access contracts and fire-system agreements, together with the dates they expire. An automated tracker also tells the right people before anything lapses.

Can Power Automate send reminder emails from an Excel file?

Yes. A scheduled cloud flow can read a table in an Excel workbook stored on OneDrive or SharePoint, decide which rows need attention and send emails through Outlook. In this tracker one flow per building system runs every morning at 8:00 AM.

How does the escalation work?

Each item moves through three stages as its expiry date approaches: Requested when it enters its reminder window, Urgent when expiry is seven days away and Alert on or after the expiry date. Each stage has a first notice and a gentler daily follow-up, giving six alert levels in total, and the follow-ups continue until a new date is recorded.

How does the tracker avoid sending unnecessary emails?

Before emailing, each row is checked in order: an On Hold switch pauses it, a scheduled contractor visit pauses it until that date, and a collected sample pauses it during the report turnaround. A last-notified date stops the same item being emailed twice in a day, and all affected areas of a system are grouped into one email.

What happens when a contractor collects a sample but never sends the report?

Once the agreed turnaround period passes without a report, the tracker sends a separate Report Overdue email asking the contractor to issue the report, rather than asking them to collect another sample.

Is the tracker in real use?

Yes. It went live in the first week of July 2026 and has run every morning since with zero errors to date.

Does it need any new software?

No. It is built entirely on Microsoft 365 tools the organisation already licenses: Excel Online, OneDrive, Power Automate and Outlook. It costs nothing extra and the maintenance team can look after it themselves.
